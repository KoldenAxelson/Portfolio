Live · predict, then teach, then check

Twenty-three people are in a room. How likely is it that two of them share a birthday?

One obvious step at a time

  1. A match can come from any two people, so count pairs, not people.
  2. Twenty-three people make 23 × 22 ÷ 2 = 253 pairs.
  3. Any one pair shares a birthday about 1 time in 365.
  4. All 253 pairs missing is 364/365 multiplied by itself 253 times, which is about one half.
  5. Worked out exactly, the chance of a shared birthday is 50.7%.

Same idea, new cue. A bike lock has 1,000 codes and 40 people each pick one at random. Do two of them probably share a code?

Forty people make 780 pairs, each matching 1 time in 1,000, so a shared code comes out at about 55%. The 4% guess counts people instead of pairs.

Both answers are exact arithmetic, not estimates: 50.7% for the birthdays and 54.6% for the lock. The second question hides the same idea behind an unfamiliar cue, which is the point of practice in the harness: recall that has to work outside the example it was taught on.
