The Kharsa is the interior of [Kretavia](/dagea/regions/kretavia/) — everything the
green coast and the western mountains do not enclose, short of the deep south-east.
It is the desert proper, and the larger part of the country by a wide margin. Most of
what Kretavia sells abroad is made here, and almost nobody outside the country has
seen it made.

## The land

It is not all sand. Much of the Kharsa is flat stone pavement and gravel, hard going
on the feet and easy going on a cart, broken by dune country in the west and by low
ridges that run for days without offering shade. Summer heat is the obvious danger and
the night cold is the one that surprises people; a caravan hand carries a blanket in
July and is not being cautious. In late spring the ghubar blows, a dust wind out of the
south that turns noon the color of weak tea, closes the roads for two or three days at
a stretch, and gets into food, joints and the works of a lock.

The country is not dramatic. It kills the unprepared quietly, in ordinary weather, and
usually because somebody miscounted water.

## The cities

Zubara is the largest, an old city on a spring-fed oasis with palm gardens running out
from the walls for an hour's walk in every direction. Its smiths supply most of the
metalwork that leaves the country. Umm Sadir, four days south, is a town of looms and
dye pits, and can be identified downwind. Halwan sits where the two main roads cross
and lives on that fact: caravanserai, brokers, a market for water shares, and the best
courts in the interior.

There is no capital. The council of religious and military authorities that governs
Kretavia keeps no fixed hall; it sits where its members are, which in practice means
one of the great oasis cities in turn, and the city that has it spends the season
feeding a great many important guests.

## Water

Water in the Kharsa is owned, divided and inherited like land. A share is measured in
time rather than volume — so many water-hours of a channel's flow, counted by shadow,
which is why arguments about the placement of a marker post can run for a generation.
The well-courts settle these, and their rulings carry further than most of what a
temple says. Well-keepers are appointed by the city and answer to the court, not to the
family that owns the share.

Against all of this sits a custom nobody has ever seen broken: a traveler at a well
drinks. There is no price and no exception, not for a stranger, a debtor or a Feldian.
What the drinker owes is an account — where he came from, what the road was like, who
he passed and what they were carrying. News moves across the Kharsa a great deal faster
than goods do.

## The roads

Two roads carry most of the traffic. The Slow Water Road follows the river down from
the coast at Sabikha to Zubara, and can be walked by anyone with sense. The Ember Road
runs east from Halwan through Umm Sadir toward the last wells before
[the Sundered Sand](/dagea/regions/the-sundered-sand/), and is not for the inexperienced.
Distances are given in skins rather than in days — a four-skin stage is a four-skin
stage whether you are fast or slow — and in summer the stages are walked at night.

## Work

The forges and the looms are the point. Kretavic metalwork and cloth are as good as
anything made in the north, and both are made in a country with no fuel: charcoal comes
down the Green Water from [the Old Wood](/dagea/regions/the-old-wood/) and is hauled
inland at a price that would astonish a Feldian smith. Salt is cut from the pans east
of Halwan. Farming is confined to the irrigated ground — dates, grain, vegetables — and
never produces a surplus worth exporting.

The standing army is quartered here, in garrison towns placed on the roads rather than
on the borders, because in the Kharsa the road is the border. Soldiers dig wells,
escort caravans and are visible everywhere, which is the intended effect.
