I've spent most of my career getting good at the work and assuming that would
be enough. Lately I have a growing suspicion it isn't. The people who get the
interesting jobs, clients and introductions are often the ones other people
already know, and I've done very little to be one of them.

These are my notes on four videos that each take a different room: online, a
dinner table, a pitch, and the office. They're a synthesis of other people's
arguments, three of them from people with a coaching program to sell, so I've
kept what held up and said where I'd push back. One idea runs through all
four: people pick who they already know and trust, and being good is what gets
you considered once they do.

| Room | From | The move |
|------|------|----------|
| Online | A founder who builds personal brands | Own a few ideas and repeat them everywhere |
| The table | Keith Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone* | Host instead of attending |

| The office | A former investment-bank employee | Be seen, and get it in writing |

## 1 · Online: own a few ideas

The brand video opens with a definition I keep coming back to: your brand is
what people say about you when you're not in the room. You have one either
way, so the only choice is whether you shape it. Shaping starts with clarity:
who you're for, and what they're struggling with, learned by actually asking
them.

From there the narrator boils it down to four content categories, the
"drumbeat" people come back for. On top of those sits what they call content
IP: the recurring elements and coined words that make work recognisably
yours, like Casey Neistat's sunglasses or Peter McKinnon's greeting. Name your
processes and you own them. I have one by accident already, the
[OX Stack](/colophon), and I've never once used it on purpose.

The practical advice is to pick two platforms, start with written ones (they
suggest X and LinkedIn, because there's no gear to buy), and post every day.
Their target is twice daily, which I'd argue is a lot for anyone with a day
job. The trick that makes any cadence possible is the content waterfall: make
one pillar piece and break it down into everything else.

The rest is housekeeping. Batch the work (fifteen videos in a day beats one a
day), look at what's resonating and do more of it, and give each post one
clear next step for the reader. The video goes on to building a team of
editors and writers, which is a later problem than mine.

## 2 · The table: host instead of attending

Keith Ferrazzi wrote *Never Eat Alone*, and his video asks how he'd rebuild his
network from nothing in a new city. First he rules out working the room and
sending a hundred LinkedIn requests, which builds a thousand contacts and
nobody who'd pick up at midnight.

Instead, write down a goal for the year and the 25 or so people who could open
that door. Find them by working backward to the real decision makers, by
raiding the network you already have (school, old coworkers, friends of
friends), and by borrowing other people's lists, like a city's "40 under 40."
Then find your anchor tenant: the most connected
person on the list who already has some tie to you. Lead with something
genuinely useful to them. Once they say yes to dinner, everyone else does too.

The dinner is six to ten people, invited a month out, and it does not need to
be fancy. He told one mentee with a studio apartment to put a cheap card table
over the coffee table and serve rotisserie chicken from the deli. The format
is a toast and a check-in: everyone shares what matters most right now,
personally and professionally, and the host goes first with something real.
His claim is that vulnerability is what turns a table of strangers into a
group, and that the host becomes the connector every later introduction runs
through.

Run it again monthly, maybe with a co-host, and keep a master list scored by
priority:

| Priority | Who | How often |
|----------|-----|-----------|
| 1 | The people who matter most to where you're going | A small touch every month |
| 2 | People you want to stay close to | A few times a year |
| 3 | Everyone else | Holidays and birthdays |

He calls the touches pings: a text, a forwarded article, a comment on a post.
Mix them up, because people need to bump into your name a few different ways
before it sticks.

## 3 · The pitch: need, then relationship, then product

Simon Squibb opens with "like it or not, you are a salesperson," and backs it
with 30 years of selling, including an agency, Fluid, that he sold to PwC. His
whole method rests on honesty. A Mini salesperson once told his wife she
should probably buy a Tesla, and that's the person they'd buy from next.
As a teenager working a summer in a clothing shop, he told a customer a suit
didn't look good on them, and they trusted him from then on.

His three steps are in a fixed order, and most people start with the last one.
First, find out whether they need it, and if not now, when. Second, build a
relationship where you like each other. Only third do you get to "here's my
product, here's what it costs." He says he's never failed the third step when
the first two were in place, and that staying present until the need shows up
is the job: he contacted one big brand every month for nine years.

The rule that stuck with me most is about promising. Split every pitch into
what you can guarantee and what you only hope will happen, then say both out
loud. The PR firms he kept working with were the ones that did exactly that.

{{< getting-known/promise >}}

The other steps are shorter. Sell in whatever style is natural to you: his
best salesperson was his accountant, who just talked about the company she
believed in over lunch with other CFOs. Train everyone around you to do the
same. One good-name client opens doors for the rest (he did free work to land
CNN, then led every call with it). Sell the outcome, which he calls the sizzle
over the steak, and only sell what you believe in. The "emotional sale," asking
people to back a mission, works only if the mission is real; otherwise people
see through it and the brand is gone.

## 4 · The office: be seen, get it in writing

Every office I've worked in has been engineers with their heads down, no drama
to speak of, and no obvious way to get ahead. The office-politics video comes
from someone who says they spent ten years at Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley
type firms, and its premise is uncomfortable: keeping your head down and hoping
the work gets noticed is a strategy, and usually a losing one.

The most useful advice is also the most boring. Vague instructions get pinned
down before you start: ask about the tasks, the criteria and the outcome, then
send a short email saying what you agreed and by when. The narrator tells a
story where a new manager tried to put them on a performance plan for following
the old manager's instructions, and the emails ended it.

{{< getting-known/recap >}}

The same goes for promotions. If you're told you're "eligible next year," ask
exactly what you need to show, and if the goalposts move after you hit them,
believe the pattern. They also watched a team post record profits and then lose
half its people, which is the case for knowing your market rate, keeping your
skills current, and treating company praise as nice but separate from
security. When onboarding is thin, keep your own map of who knows what; people
on other teams, who don't see you as competition, tend to be the most helpful.

I'm leaving out about a third of the video. It also recommends pretending to
agree with whoever has power, letting a rival walk into a mistake, and assuming
coworkers are jealous. That might describe a trading floor, but it doesn't
describe any team I'd want to be on.

## The plan

Simon Squibb's three steps turn out to fit the whole page. Getting good at the
work is step three, the product. Steps one and two are people, and those are
the ones I've been skipping. Here's my plan:

1. Write down the goal for this year and the 25 names, and find the anchor
   tenant.
2. Pick two platforms. Every article I publish becomes a waterfall instead of a
   single link.
3. Host one dinner, even if it's a card table and a rotisserie chicken.
4. Keep the priority list and send the pings.
5. When I pitch anything, start with need, then the relationship, and split the
   promise into what I guarantee and what I hope.
6. At work, send a recap after every vague ask, and get promotion criteria in
   writing.

Four videos, watched in October 2026. Everything above is my synthesis of
their arguments, and the figures (followers, revenue, years, the PwC sale, the

pitches. The brand and office videos don't give their narrators' names, so I
