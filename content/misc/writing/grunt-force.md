Grunt Force is the novel I'm writing. It follows four guys on a jobs-program base in 2042, in a country that fixed real problems one sensible policy at a time until the fixes became the trap. These are my working notes, so expect drafts, placeholders and open questions. The manuscript itself will stay sealed until it's done.

## The World {#world}

*The Grunt Force: World-Building Bible*

> **Dignity without Autonomy.**

### Core Theme: The Banality of Evil

This is not a story about an evil America. It's a story about a country that tried to solve real problems (job displacement, housing, debt, crime, trauma) and kept optimizing for its own betterment until the solutions became the horror.

- **No cartoon villains.** Every policy has a sympathetic origin and a reasonable-sounding justification.
- **Nobody planned the worst outcomes.** The algorithm optimizes, contractors cut costs, officials hit quotas. Harm emerges from incentives, not malice.
- **Most people involved believe they're helping**, or at least that they're doing their job.
- **From the outside, it works.** Low crime, no homelessness, happy influencers, full employment. The horror is only visible from inside, and often only in hindsight.

#### Real problem → policy → result

| Real struggle | The fix | What it became |
|---|---|---|
| AI outpaces human workers | Grunt Force: "dignity through work" | Lifelong labor with no mobility |
| Housing crisis | Container homes, free of rent | Tiny, uniform, assigned housing |
| Debt crushing families | Debt forgiven on enlistment | A path into the system for the desperate |
| Overcrowded prisons | Plea deals into service | A captive labor pool |
| Fairness in job assignment | Algorithmic social trust score | A hidden number that decides who's expendable |
| Disabled/injured workers given bad jobs | Sergeant score override | A tool for punishment and favoritism |
| Veteran and worker PTSD | Neural chip "Trauma Relief" | People who can no longer be horrified |
| Medical progress needs testers | Chip-Jobs and drug trials | Grunts as beta testers for human minds |
| Postwar devastation | Beautification campaigns | Body recovery as a work assignment |

### The Grunt Force

#### Timeline and setting

- **Story year: 2042.** The sitting President is in his second term.
- **The Grunt Force was created in his first term**, so it's established but still new, a few years old.
- **Rapid growth.** Enlistment is outpacing construction; some Grunts live in temporary dormitories while waiting for housing.
- **Bases in most states.** States without one are building one, since bases are widely profitable for the host state. **Hawaii is the exception:** the land is too valuable.
- **The story's base is in New Mexico**, now renamed Old Mexico, making it the closest base to the Beautification zone.

#### Overview

A new branch of the federal government, structured like a military branch but not a fighting force. Created when AI overtook humans in most sectors and the government stepped in to give displaced citizens "dignity through work."

- **Feel:** summer camp meets military scheduling.
- **No upward mobility.** A Grunt is always a Grunt.
- **Entry is a choice**, though sometimes a cornered one.
- **Price of entry: sterilization.** Members can't have children. Pets are encouraged and subsidized.
- **Unspoken function:** the communal lifestyle (no rent, shared meals, guaranteed work, no competition) attracts people who wanted that life anyway, quietly concentrating them where they're fed, watched, and not reproducing. Nobody in charge says this out loud.

#### Who joins (all on the same mixed bases)

- **Volunteers.** True believers, the burned out, people who wanted off the treadmill.
- **Plea-dealers.** Nonviolent and low-level offenders who chose sterilization and service over prison. Violent offenders are deemed unfit (and the worst are executed).
- **Debt enlistees.** Joined to have medical or student debt forgiven.
- **Displaced workers.** People, sometimes whole towns, whose industries were automated.

Intake type is **not visibly marked**. Only the system and Sergeants know for certain. Grunts guess from behavior: who never takes leave, who has extra check-ins, who has curfew. This lets some plea-dealers pass as volunteers. Housing is assigned, so a true believer may live next to a former shoplifter.

### The Base

- **Housing:** rows of shipping-container-sized homes (about 320 sq ft) with white picket fences and tiny yards. The American Dream aesthetic, shrunk down. Roughly 15–20 units per acre.
- **Kitchenette** for heating vendor meals.
- **Bases** of roughly 50,000–150,000 people, built on decommissioned military land, dead malls, and former prison towns.
- **Scale precedent:** the New Deal CCC (about 300,000 at a time, about 3 million total), the WPA (about 8.5 million employed), today's DoD (about 1.3 million active duty).
- **Funding:** an automation dividend tax on AI companies. The machines pay for human dignity.

#### Amenities (the honey)

- No rent, no bills, no debt
- Free healthcare, dental, and counseling (counselors report to the system)
- Subsidized pets, vet care, dog parks
- Gyms, pools, gardens, game rooms, movie nights, mess halls
- Classes and hobbies: painting, music, woodworking, book clubs. Endless self-improvement with nowhere to use it.
- Clean, safe, crime-free streets. It looks like a postcard.

#### Third spaces vs. isolation

- **Shared amenities are immaculate**, since so much pointless work is maintenance and upkeep. The Commons, mess hall, game lounges, and pools always look brand new.
- **Many Grunts barely use them.** They stay in their tiny homes, absorbed in screens. The beautiful shared spaces can sit half empty.
- **Gaming culture is huge.** The honeypot draws comfort-seeking, somewhat hedonistic people, and compulsive gaming is common. The system encourages it: entertained people are calm people.
- **Online squads** form across the base, groups of Grunts who game together nightly from separate homes and may rarely meet in person.

### Sergeants

- Manage the Grunts but are **not Grunt Force members** themselves (or are, but unsterilized and trained in another branch).
- Can have families, live in bigger housing or off-base, come and go freely.
- **Know and can see Grunt scores. Can rewrite them.**
- Range from kind and trapped to petty tyrants.
- **Typically men**, physically imposing. Keeping people in line relies on implied force, never stated.
- **Know every Grunt's intake status.** Grunts don't volunteer whether they're plea-dealers, so Sergeants hold dirt on everyone. They rarely use it; the possibility is enough.
- The only way to a Sergeant's life is to never join at all.

### The Social Trust Score

- Every citizen has one. **Only the system knows the number.**
- Determines eligibility for jobs and perks.
- The algorithm tracks talent and satisfaction: if you enjoy an easy job, you'd better be good at it to keep it.
- Grunts read their score indirectly through assignments, leading to paranoia, self-policing, and superstition ("don't complain at dinner, the walls listen").

#### Score tiers

- **High:** real, meaningful work (harvests, disaster response, elite event staffing), premium food vendors, Chip-Jobs.
- **Low:** pointless busywork (repainting clean fences, re-sorting sorted bins, digging holes that get filled).
- **Bottom:** quietly selected for hazardous work and trials. Nobody is told why.

#### Sergeant override

- **Original purpose:** safety. Flag a Grunt as unfit for an assignment (injury, blindness, illness).
- **Loophole:** the system can't tell a medical override from a punitive one. The reason field is never checked. Audits are rare and done by people with the same incentives.
- **Abuses:** punishment, favoritism, bribery (food, goods, favors), filling trial quotas, covering up injuries after the fact.
- **Kindness and abuse look identical in the logs.**

### Work

Members don't apply. The algorithm posts assignments daily. No refusing, swapping, or specializing. A gifted carpenter may never touch wood again. Sergeants enforce the schedule but don't set it.

**Most work for low scorers is pointless, and that's the product.** Tired, fed, entertained people don't riot.

#### On-base

Grounds and landscaping, constant fence painting, kitchens, laundry, cleaning, pet care, hand-sorting recycling.

#### Labor Bus (off-base)

- Delicate harvests where robots bruise fruit
- Disaster and wildfire cleanup
- Hazardous work (asbestos, toxic sites); people are cheaper to replace than robots
- Clearing highways and power lines
- "Human-touch" service at elite events

#### Human-required work

- **Sign-off clerks:** approving AI decisions so a human carries legal liability
- **Training data:** rating AI and demonstrating tasks for robots, training their replacements
- **Companionship:** scheduled visits to nursing homes
- **Product testing:** food, mattresses, ergonomics, drugs

#### How the government benefits

- **Contracting:** Grunt labor sold cheaply to private contractors, pushing down outside wages and pushing more people into the Grunt Force. Self-feeding.
- **Emergency workforce:** millions on call, no unions, no strikes. (Precedent: incarcerated firefighters in California.)
- **Political currency:** Labor Buses handed to cooperative politicians' districts as favors.
- **Showcase projects:** monuments and parks "built by the people."
- **Data:** constant behavioral monitoring, valuable for training human-management AI.
- **Stability:** keeps millions of displaced people busy, calm, and childless.

### The Chip

A neural-link-style brain chip. Common among the public for convenience (hands-free devices, focus, sleep). The chip isn't scary. The features are.

- **New features need human testing.** Grunts are a large, monitored population with a documented "need." After A/B testing on a few thousand Grunts, features roll out to the public.
- **A/B tests and placebos.** Nobody knows which version they got.
- **Over-the-air updates.** The chip can change after installation, without surgery or notice.
- **Getting chipped earns points but isn't forced.** It's costly and must be earned.

#### Two paths

- **Earned:** high scorers save up for a stable, approved version. A status symbol.
- **Trial:** low scorers get experimental versions free through the "need" pool.

The people who most need help can only get the riskiest version.

#### The catch-22

- You need points to get the chip; the chip gives you points.
- Suffering lowers your score, putting the safe chip out of reach and the experimental one within reach.
- Refusing isn't punished officially, but your score keeps sliding and the next offer comes sooner.
- Earned chips get the same updates as everyone else eventually.

#### Possible test features

Emotional dampening, focus and anti-boredom, compliance ("impulse control"), pain tolerance, and **satisfaction**: contentment regardless of circumstances. If the system can make people content with anything, it no longer needs honey.

#### The PTSD "cure"

1. A Grunt returns from body recovery traumatized.
2. The algorithm reads it as poor performance; score drops.
3. Low score qualifies them for "experimental trauma treatment."
4. They return calm, cheerful, productive. Score recovers.

**The existential horror:** they remember everything. They simply no longer have a trauma response, now or in the future. They can recover bodies unfazed. Their trauma was the proof they saw victims as people; their humanity was the symptom. They don't miss it, because the part that would grieve is gone.

- The cured become an ideal body-recovery workforce with rising scores.
- The system now benefits from traumatizing people. Nobody planned it; the algorithm optimizes.
- Some Grunts eventually seek the cure preemptively.
- The public version is marketed as "Trauma Relief" to veterans and first responders.

#### Chip-Jobs

- **The pitch:** let the chip run in the background for weeks, stay home, game, eat, earn points. The cushiest job there is.
- **The reality:** every Chip-Job is a trial. Possible background tasks: feature testing, ad and propaganda reaction testing, AI training on human brain responses, compute, sleep research.
- **Grunts rate them highly**, so the algorithm assigns more. They choose their way deeper in.
- **Outsiders see Chip-Job Grunts as lucky.**
- **Not permanent:** a month or two of "Chipping-Away," then back on the bus.

#### The rotation

1. Chipping-Away: comfort, games, rest.
2. Labor Bus south: rubble and remains.
3. Return traumatized; score drops.
4. Treatment offer, which is another Chip-Job.

The cozy weeks make the horror bearable; the horror makes the comfort feel earned. Some Grunts feel guilty about the easy months, as if the bodies were the price.

### Daily Life

#### Food

- Factor-style prepared meals heated in the kitchenette, from a handful of vendors (fictional brands in the book).
- **Pay to play:** companies pay the government for a spot on the approved list.
- **Competition within the list:** Grunts rate meals; low-rated vendors lose their slot.
- **The squeeze:** vendors must recoup fees, so they cut costs to the bone while staying just better than competitors. Shrinkflation.
- Grunts develop real brand loyalties and arguments.
- Higher scores may unlock premium vendors or extra credits.

#### Internet and media

- Full internet and entertainment in every home.
- **Post-digital-ID world.** Major platforms require ID posting.
- **Posting as yourself** shows a Grunt tag. **Posting anonymously** carries stigma.
- Most Grunts consume and stay silent.
- **Anonymous underground:** where real talk happens (score theories, trial rumors), easily dismissed by outsiders as conspiracy.
- **Grunt influencers:** cheerful "day in the life" creators, possibly boosted by the government. The public face of the Grunt Force.
- **Family calls:** easy, encouraged, monitored. Conversations stay light.
- Nobody has to censor anything; the incentives handle it.

#### Leave

- **Generous:** around 30 days a year plus holidays, more than most Americans outside get.
- Travel discounts and free buses to visit family.
- **The rumor:** long leaves hurt your score. Nobody confirms it. People self-limit and come back early.
- Late returns are a guaranteed score hit.
- **Plea-dealers get no personal leave.** Only "destination labor" on Beautification projects (cleaning Hawaiian beaches, restoring national parks), marketed as a privilege. In paradise, but not allowed to touch it.

#### Desertion

Treated the same as in any military branch. **Dignity comes with standards.**

### The Outside World

Glimpses of the outside world come back to the base through returning crews, family calls, anonymous posts, and the changed behavior of the cured.

#### The war with Mexico

- A brief but bloody war in **spring 2042**, after the main characters have already arrived on base. Swift and devastating. The US pushes its border south to Panama.
- Beautification begins right after, so recovery crews deal with **fresh bodies**.
- New Mexico becomes Old Mexico after the war, and Grunts on maintenance duty repaint the signs.

#### The justification

Each argument is reasonable on its own, partly true, and easy to nod along to. That's what makes it work.

- **Efficiency.** Defending a narrow strip of land at Panama costs less effort and fewer taxes than defending a long southern border.
- **The Canal.** America built it, so America should control and profit from the trade through it.
- **Governance.** Mexico was failing to govern: cartels, human trafficking, and drug trafficking ran unchecked.
- **A localized Monroe Doctrine.** The hemisphere is America's responsibility, so start close to home.

#### Expansion context (off-screen)

Canada, Greenland, Iceland, and Cuba are already the 51st through 54th states. **Mexico is the 55th**, fitting for Cinco de Mayo (5/5). By the time of the war, the public is used to the map growing, so one more state feels like a natural step, not a shock.
- Everything north of Panama that wasn't already American is now just **Mexico**, a state. Simpler than absorbing distinct cultures, and Americans are bad at geography anyway.
- **New Mexico is renamed Old Mexico.** Nobody can give directions anymore. Mail goes to the wrong one. Old Mexico residents are furious about the name alone.
- **Grunt slang:** "shipped to Old" means a cushy rebuild job; "shipped to Mexico" means body recovery.

#### Beautification of Mexico

- Officially: "restoring the new state," with cheerful posters and tree-planting photo ops.
- Really: rubble, remains, mass burials, sorting belongings. Children's toys and family photos are what break people.
- High scorers get visible rebuilding work. Low scorers and plea-dealers get body recovery.

#### The annexed population

Handled offhand, as the Grunts handle it. Survivors go to **Assimilation Camps** to learn English and American culture, or leave for South America. Ways it surfaces:

- A Sergeant's briefing: "The locals have all been processed."
- A Grunt grumbling that Assimilation Camps get better food.
- Propaganda of smiling graduates on a mess hall TV nobody watches.
- An empty house with belongings but no bodies. A Grunt almost wonders where the family went, then moves to the next house.

### Story Threads and Images

- The Labor Bus: beach trip one day, drug trial the next.
- Fresh white paint on fences that were already clean.
- A Grunt can't see their score, and can't prove a Sergeant tanked it.
- A decent Sergeant fakes an injury flag to protect someone, and the system notices the fast "recovery."
- A cured Grunt describes recovering a child's body in the tone of describing lunch.
- A cured partner who would react the same way to your death.
- A failed chip version rolls back, and everything suppressed hits at once.
- A Chip-Job ends and a hobby you loved just doesn't interest you anymore.
- A plea-dealer on a Hawaiian beach, considering running.
- An outsider happily buys "Trauma Relief," unaware of where it was perfected.
- A narrator weighing the cure: is relief worth becoming someone who'll never suffer again?

### Open Questions

- Do the Sergeants have chips? Is going unchipped a mark of standing above the system?
- Can the chip be removed?
- Does the government know the cure removes more than trauma, or does it just like the numbers?
- How do the cured get along with each other?
- What does the public at large believe about the Grunt Force?

## Characters and Plotlines {#characters}

*Characters and Plotlines (Draft 3)*

> **Status:** names, ages, and details are placeholders to react to and revise.

**Setting:** Grunt Force base, Old Mexico (formerly New Mexico), 2042. The book spans roughly one year. The guys arrive before the war; the war breaks out after they've settled in.

### The Group: GruntSquad

Four guys who would never have met outside the Grunt Force. Their only real connections are gaming, the base, and fraternal banter.

- **Xbox lobby energy.** Trash talk, crude jokes, ribbing each other about everything including race, and a loyalty none of them would ever say out loud. The kind of group that's accidentally more diverse than almost any other in America.
- **Recognizable types, real people.** Each guy starts close to a familiar type, which makes the banter land. The story then goes deeper than the type: the reasons behind it and the cost of it.
- **Banter as a tool.** The jokes show affection and keep pain at arm's length. When the banter stops, or one guy stops joking about a certain subject, the reader knows something is wrong.
- **The lobby as barometer.** Who's online, who's late, who stopped logging in.
- **Everyone is running from something.** Different reasons, different struggles, same picket fences.

### Opening Chapter: The Fliers

**Narrator:** Owen.

Owen's assignment of the week is office work, and he has temporary printer access. He prints fliers for a gaming meetup ("Looking for squad. Commons game lounge, Thursday 1900.") and spends the chapter walking the base posting them, delighted to be building community the way the Grunt Force promised.

What the walk shows, without explaining:
- Rows of container homes and freshly painted picket fences
- Grunts on maintenance duty, repainting what's already clean
- The morning assignment board
- Pets everywhere
- Dorms for overflow newcomers
- The Commons: beautiful, immaculate, and mostly empty
- New Mexico state signage, still unchanged. (After the war, the guys watch crews paint "Old" over "New.")

At the meetup location, three guys show up: Andre, Kevin, and Mateo. Before the first game finishes, **Sgt. Reyes** arrives and reprimands Owen for unauthorized use of printing resources. Small infraction, delivered by the book. The four bond over the absurdity of it.

Owen walks home wondering whether it cost him points. **First seed of the hidden score.**

### Narrators

#### Owen Hale — The True Believer
- **Age:** 28. **Background:** white, upper-middle-class suburbs. **Intake:** volunteer.
- **Type:** the online political-head, a self-described communist who argued about it for years.
- **Running from:** parents who saw him as a disappointment. He joined because the Grunt Force looked like the system he'd always argued for.
- **Why it matters:** he desperately needs the Grunt Force to work, because if it doesn't, his whole worldview, and his reason for walking away from his family, falls with it.
- **In the squad:** the organizer and the guy who won't stop talking politics. The others roast him for it constantly.
- **Voice:** earnest, wordy, quick to theorize, notices community and structure.
- **Arc:**
  - *Beginning:* the Grunt Force is proof he was right. His narration glows.
  - *Turn:* watches what the system does to Mateo, Andre, and Kevin, and finds that it fits his ideals on paper while betraying them in practice.
  - *End:* disillusioned. The biggest fall in the book, because he had the furthest to fall. Decides whether to defend it anyway, speak out under his Grunt ID, or go home.

#### Andre Lewis — The Plea-Dealer
- **Age:** 25. **Background:** Black, from a rough neighborhood. **Intake:** plea deal.
- **Type:** came up around gang life; loves GTA.
- **Running from:** gang violence. He figured the Grunt Force was better than the hood, got wrapped up in trouble, and took the plea specifically to get out of that life.
- **Twist:** before taking the plea, he fathered a child without knowing. The sterilization was supposed to end that chapter of his life, but he already has a kid out there.
- **In the squad:** the funniest guy, loudest on voice chat, plays GTA while the rest play whatever Owen picked.
- **Voice:** confident, quick, guarded, notices guards and who's watching.
- **Arc:**
  - *Beginning:* relief. Safe for the first time in years. Restrictions feel like a fair trade.
  - *Turn:* learns he has a kid (a message through a family call or a tagged post). As a plea-dealer, he gets no personal leave and can't go see his child. Sent on destination labor to Hawaii, the farthest from home he's ever been.
  - *End:* weighs desertion to see his kid, knowing desertion carries military consequences. The sterilization meant to remove fatherhood didn't. Only the system's rules stand between him and his child.

#### Kevin Lin — The Failed Doctor
- **Age:** 30. **Background:** Chinese American, immigrant parents. **Intake:** plea deal, **passing as a volunteer.**
- **His cover:** tells everyone he's just a guy who couldn't make it in med school. Never mentions the drug charge.
- **Why it holds:** plea-dealers get no personal leave, but Kevin never asks for leave. He doesn't want to see his family, and they don't want to see him. The one restriction that would expose him is one he'd never use.
- **Type:** the tiger-parent kid. "Why you no doctor?"
- **Running from:** expectations he couldn't meet. Got into med school, fell behind, and sold prescription drugs he got through school. Expelled. Joining and being sterilized ended the family line, his own final act of disappointment, and maybe of defiance.
- **Unique angle:** his medical training means he understands more than anyone else about the drug trials, the chip, and what the "cure" might really do.
- **In the squad:** the smart one, the cynic, the quiet worst-case thinker.
- **Voice:** dry, precise, self-deprecating, clinical about things that should hurt.
- **Arc:**
  - *Beginning:* hiding. No expectations here; it's a relief to be no one.
  - *Turn:* lands a run of Chip-Jobs, the dream assignment. He's the one who could recognize what they are, and he chooses not to look too closely, because they're comfortable.
  - *End:* slowly stops loving games, his one real joy, and notices too late. Knowing better didn't protect him.

#### Mateo Ruiz — The Debt Enlistee
- **Age:** 33. **Background:** second-generation Mexican American; grew up going back and forth between both countries. **Intake:** debt.
- **Type:** the bright, upwardly mobile son of immigrants. A nice college, a mortgage, real prospects.
- **Running from:** a life that fell apart in a moment. A cartel gunman mistook him for a target and shot him. He survived brain surgery but now depends on a medical chip to stay alive and stable, a medical marvel. The debt for surgery, plus loans and mortgage, was more than he could ever pay. The Grunt Force forgave it all.
- **Key detail:** **he already has a chip.** He's the only one of the four chipped from day one, for medical reasons.
- **In the squad:** the steady one, the big brother, worst at games and good-natured about it.
- **Voice:** warm, thoughtful, bilingual flashes, notices family and home.
- **Arc:**
  - *Beginning:* grieving the life he lost, grateful to be alive.
  - *War:* when the war starts, he has complicated feelings. Cartels nearly killed him, so part of him supports it. Part of him remembers the places and people on the other side.
  - *Turn:* rotated south to recovery, clearing fresh bodies. A family looks like his own, like the people he visited growing up. He returns with PTSD; his score falls.
  - *The cure:* because his chip is already installed, the "treatment" is just a **software update**. No surgery, maybe no real consent beyond a tap of approval.
  - *End:* "cured." **Told only through glimpses after the update.** His jokes are still there; something behind them isn't.

### Supporting Characters

#### Sgt. Tomás Reyes — The Insider (1–2 chapters)
- **Age:** 41. Army-trained, unsterilized, two kids off-base. Grew up in New Mexico. Big, physically imposing; authority that rests on implied force.
- **Worldview:** believes he's doing God's work. The Grunts couldn't make it in the real world, so he gives them dignity by doing the thinking they couldn't do for themselves.
- **Doesn't trust their discipline:** if they had it, they wouldn't be here. His strictness is a kind of pity.
- **Manner:** a strict father with drill-sergeant condescension. Never cruel for its own sake; he believes every reprimand is for their good. That sincerity is what makes him dangerous.
- **Knows everyone's intake status**, including Kevin's. Like all Sergeants, he rarely uses it, but everyone suspects the Sergeants hold dirt on them.
- First appears reprimanding Owen over the fliers, strictly by the book.
- **Arc:** pressured to fill the monthly trial quota. Fakes an injury flag to protect Mateo before his rotation, and the system flags it. Fills the quota anyway. The reader sees whom she chooses.

#### Andre's girlfriend (name TBD)
- A fellow Grunt on base. Sterility doesn't end desire, and on-base relationships are common, easy, and consequence-free by design.
- May be with Andre from the start or meet him during the story.
- **Tension:** she represents the life he can have here. His child's mother represents the life outside he can't reach. Learning about the kid strains both relationships.

#### Andre's child and the child's mother
- Mostly off-screen. Known through calls and photos.
- The most powerful outside window into the world the guys gave up.

#### Kevin's parents
- Possibly never seen. Silence as presence.

#### Owen's parents
- A single strained family call could carry a whole chapter.

#### Cole — The Hider
- A fifth lobby regular who joins later. Quiet on voice chat.
- Concealed a violent past to get in. Clues appear across the guys' chapters; revealed late.

#### Kenzie — The Influencer
- Cheerful "day in the life" creator. Covers Statehood Day on camera.

### Climax (Draft): Killing Cole

**Core idea:** Cole is the antagonist. The four plan to kill him before he can kill them, in self-defense, more or less. Beating the antagonist is the crime that turns the setting against them.

#### Build-up
- Cole joins the lobby later; quiet, watchful.
- Clues to his violent past surface across the guys' chapters.
- **The tournament.** A local base gaming tournament. Cole gets humiliated in a ranked match by the squad, and online trash talk escalates.
- **Killing the dog.** In revenge, Cole kills **Mateo's dog** and makes it look like an accident. The reader has learned to love this dog through the setup chapters; for Mateo and his wife, it stood in for the kids they never had.
- **The glitch.** The dog dies after Mateo's cure. His grief is a different emotion than the trauma the chip was built to suppress, and strong enough to break through. For a moment he's overwhelmed: sobbing, shaking, grief beyond anything the squad has seen from him, possibly with a physical sign like a nosebleed or bleeding from the eyes as the chip strains. Then it shuts off. He becomes cold, calm, and collected, perhaps more detached than Cole.
- **Seen from outside.** Since Mateo no longer narrates, the glitch is witnessed in another narrator's chapter. Kevin, with his medical training, may be the one who recognizes what he just watched.
- **The system notices.** The chip logs the event. A patch may follow.
- **Mateo drives the plan.** After the glitch, Mateo becomes the calmest and most efficient planner of Cole's death. The man the system "cured" of trauma is the one who makes killing possible.
- **Cole's view:** a psychopath who doesn't see the big deal. A new dog is just paperwork here. His logic is the system's own logic about pets (subsidized, interchangeable, a pacifier), taken to its end.
- **Escalation.** The guys suspect, then know. Cole learns something that could ruin them (Andre's plan to see his kid, Kevin's plea status) and becomes a real threat: blackmail, intimidation, hints he'd hurt them next. The dog is proof he's capable. **Keep it ambiguous** whether he would have killed them.

#### The crime
- Each guy's skills make it possible: Kevin's medical knowledge, Mateo's clean record and good score, Owen's planning, Andre's experience with violence.
- The lobby becomes their alibi, accounts online while they're elsewhere.
- Each guy's reason to go along is different, and each is partly self-serving.

#### The turn
- The system knew about Cole's past all along. Sergeants have the dirt on everyone. He was let in anyway, possibly because he was useful as an informant.
- Every hidden check fires at once: scores, chip data, Sergeant knowledge, intake records, ID posts.

#### Ending (Draft)

**The system wins quietly. A standalone novel.**

- **The cover-up mirrors the dog.** Tomás pieces it together from chip logs, lobby activity that doesn't add up, and the dirt he already had. Cole's death is recorded as an accident, just as Cole disguised the dog's. A dead Grunt, like a dead dog, is just paperwork. Tomás believes he's sparing them, like a father covering for his sons. Now he holds their worst secret and never needs to mention it.
- **Mateo.** The glitch is patched. Reassigned to permanent recovery duty in Mexico, where he excels. A model Grunt, and a stranger to his wife.
- **Kevin.** Wracked with guilt, chooses the cure himself, knowing exactly what it does. The only character who takes it with eyes open.
- **Andre.** Granted one supervised visit with his child as a reward for cooperation. Given as a favor, withheld again after. Returns to base and his girlfriend.
- **Owen.** Narrates the final chapter. Disillusioned, implicated, and staying.
- **Last image:** bookending the opening, Owen walks past new arrivals in the overflow dorms and finds a flier someone else posted looking for a gaming squad. He leaves it up. That night the others are online in the lobby, quiet, playing together anyway. Something human survives, small and powerless, inside a system that doesn't need to fear it.

## Writing the Mosaic {#mosaic}

*Writing the Mosaic: A Style Guide for *Grunt Force**

A **mosaic novel** (or "novel in stories") tells one larger story through chapters that each center on a different character. Each chapter works on its own, but read together they form a picture no single narrator could see.

**Models to study:**
- *Olive Kitteridge* (Elizabeth Strout): one character leads some chapters and is a passing figure in others.
- *A Visit from the Goon Squad* (Jennifer Egan): interlinked lives, each chapter a different lens and even a different form.
- *World War Z* (Max Brooks): many voices assembling one world, close to the world-heavy feel of this book.
- *Winesburg, Ohio* (Sherwood Anderson): an early classic of the form, one town seen through many residents.

### Core Principles

#### 1. Every chapter is a complete story

Each chapter needs its own small arc: a want, a complication, and a change (even a small one). A reader should finish a chapter satisfied, even if bigger threads stay open.

**Test:** could this chapter be published as a short story? If not, it's leaning too hard on the chapters around it.

#### 2. One narrator per chapter

Stay in a single point of view for each chapter. The power of the form comes from limited perspectives. Each narrator misreads, misses, or misjudges something another narrator understands.

#### 3. Chronology moves forward

Chapters follow the calendar. Time can skip (weeks or months between chapters), but it doesn't go backward. Flashbacks live inside a character's memory, not as standalone chapters.

**Tip:** open each chapter with a grounding detail that tells the reader when we are: a season, an event ("two weeks after Statehood Day"), or an assignment rotation.

#### 4. Arcs continue off-screen

A character's story keeps moving when they aren't narrating. When we see them again, things have changed, and the reader has to piece together what happened.

### Techniques

#### The glimpse

A character's arc can be told largely through other people's chapters. A friend appears briefly, and the reader notices something the narrator doesn't.

> *Example:* Dale narrates early chapters before his body-recovery rotation. After his cure, we only see him in other characters' chapters: cheerful, helpful, and wrong. The reader never gets his "after" from the inside, which makes it scarier.

**Rule of thumb:** each major character should appear in at least one chapter they don't narrate, preferably at a turning point in their arc.

#### Dramatic irony between chapters

Because the reader sees more than any narrator, you can let them know something a character doesn't.

> *Example:* A Sergeant chapter reveals the trial quota. Three chapters later, a Grunt is thrilled to get a Chip-Job, and the reader knows what it is.

This is one of the strongest tools for a banality-of-evil story. The horror lives in what the reader knows that the characters don't.

#### The same event, two views

Occasionally show one moment from two narrators in different chapters. Use it sparingly, only when the second view changes its meaning.

> *Example:* Statehood Day celebrated by an influencer on camera, then the same day from a recovery crew in Mexico watching the fireworks on their phones.

#### Recurring images

Objects and images that pass between chapters tie the mosaic together. Each appearance should mean something slightly different.

Candidates from the world:
- Fresh white paint on picket fences
- The Labor Bus
- The morning assignment board
- A specific vendor meal everyone has an opinion on
- The GruntSquad lobby and who is or isn't logged in
- A child's toy recovered in Mexico

#### Echo lines

A phrase said by one character and repeated by another, in a new context. Official slogans work especially well, since their meaning sours as the book goes on.

- "Dignity comes with standards."
- "Shipped to Old" / "shipped to Mexico."
- "Dignity without autonomy."

### Voice

Each narrator should be recognizable within a paragraph, even without a name.

Distinguish narrators through:
- **Vocabulary and slang.** A former trucker, a burned-out social worker, and a 22-year-old gamer talk differently.
- **Sentence rhythm.** Long and anxious, clipped and flat, chatty and digressive.
- **What they notice.** A plea-dealer notices cameras and guards. A believer notices the flowers. A gamer notices screens and who's online.
- **What they avoid.** Every narrator has something they won't think about directly. Their avoidance is characterization.

**Exercise:** write the same short scene (the morning assignment board) in each narrator's voice before drafting chapters.

#### The cured voice

If a cured character ever narrates, their voice should be calm, pleasant, and slightly empty: fully coherent, with no emotional weight on the things that should carry it. Use this at most once, for maximum effect.

### Structure and Pacing

#### The rhythm

Mirror the Grunt rotation: alternate soft chapters (Chipping-Away, gaming, base comforts) with hard ones (Labor Bus, recovery, trials). The reader feels the whiplash the characters live with.

#### Spine events

Even a mosaic needs a few shared events that every narrator lives through. These anchor chronology and let you show contrasting reactions.

Suggested spine events for 2042:
- A big rotation to Mexico
- Statehood Day (May 5)
- A new chip feature rolling out
- A death, disappearance, or desertion on base

#### Chapter balance

- Main narrators: 3–5 chapters each.
- Minor narrators (like a Sergeant): 1–2 chapters, placed at key moments.
- Aim for the reader never going more than about 4 chapters without seeing a main character, either narrating or glimpsed.

#### The ending

Mosaic endings often work by convergence: threads that seemed separate meet in one event or realization. The final chapter can be narrated by an unexpected voice, or return to the first narrator to show how they changed.

### Tracking Tools

Keep a **thread tracker** (see the characters and plot document). For each chapter, record:

| Chapter | Narrator | Date | Narrator's arc beat | Others glimpsed | Their off-screen arc beat | Spine event? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Before drafting each chapter, check:
- Where is every main character's arc right now?
- What has the reader learned that this narrator doesn't know?
- What does this chapter change?

### Pitfalls

- **Lore dumps.** Let the world come through daily life, not explanation. Narrators don't explain things they take for granted.
- **Interchangeable voices.** If two narrators sound alike, merge them or rewrite one.
- **Disconnected chapters.** Every chapter should touch at least one thread from elsewhere in the book.
- **Over-explaining connections.** Trust the reader. A glimpse is enough; don't have a narrator point out what it means.
- **Abandoned threads.** If a character's arc starts, it needs an ending, even if the ending is shown only through a glimpse.
- **Showing everything.** Like the iceberg principle: know far more than you show.

### Drafting Approach for a First Novel

1. Finalize the cast and each character's arc (beginning, turn, end).
2. Lay out spine events on a 2042 calendar.
3. Fill in the thread tracker chapter by chapter.
4. Write voice samples for each narrator.
5. Draft in chronological order. Don't polish until the full draft exists.
6. In revision, check that every glimpse lands and every thread closes.
