Only 7% of American adults say they took part in a book club in the past
year, according to a [Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/09/americans-still-opt-for-print-books-over-digital-or-audio-versions-few-are-in-book-clubs/)
from October 2025. Women were twice as likely as men to be in one, 10% to 5%.
That's a small club for an idea at least four hundred years old, and I'd bet
plenty of the other 93% have wondered whether they'd like it.

This page answers the questions I'd want answered before joining one: what a
book club actually is, what it's good for, how to find or start one, and which
tools take the admin off your hands.

## Same book, same deadline, one room

A book club is a small group that reads the same book on a schedule and meets
to talk about it. Library guides put the comfortable size at
[6 to 12 people](https://newcastlelibraries.com.au/experience/what-s-on/book-club-hub/getting-started):
enough that a few no-shows don't sink the night, few enough that everyone gets
a turn. Most meet monthly for an hour or two, in a living room, a library room,
a café or a video call.

The name gets stretched. Oprah's Book Club, which began in 1996, and the
celebrity clubs that followed are mostly a shared reading list with a famous
person choosing. The Book-of-the-Month Club, founded in 1926, is a mail-order
service. Shared Reading, a model from the UK charity The Reader, turns the
usual format around: the group reads aloud together in the room, so nobody
arrives having done homework.

## What actually gets said

This was the part I couldn't picture. On my [Impossible List](/impossible-list/)
each book gets a star rating and a line, and I wasn't sure what I'd say about
even a favorite beyond that. It turns out the rating is where the talking
starts. Library guides suggest swapping "Did you like it?" for
["What was your reading experience?"](https://www.santacruzpl.org/files/pdf/readers/bkclb.pdf),
so the question in the room becomes why it got four stars and what kept it
from five.

Most of an evening runs on a few kinds of question:

- Where you got hooked, where you nearly quit, and which scene you read twice.
- Who you trusted, who you wanted to shake, and whether anyone changed.
- Why the author ended it there, told it from that seat, or skipped the scene
  everyone wanted.
- What it reminded you of in your own life, as much or as little as you care to
  share.
- Where you disagree. Someone loved the book you couldn't finish, and I
  suspect those make the best meetings.

Preparing is lighter than it sounds. [LitLovers](https://litlovers.com/run-a-book-club/lead-a-book-club-discussion)
suggests reading with a pencil and marking the passages that land, then
bringing them along as evidence, so you arrive with two or three page numbers
and a question you didn't get answered. Some groups also ask whoever picked the
book to say
[why they chose it](https://nlc.nebraska.gov/Ref/Bookclub/bookgroupdiscussiontips.aspx),
which gets the room going while everyone settles in.

## Sermons on a ship, 1634

The usual candidate for the first book club is a group Anne Hutchinson started
in 1634 on her way to the Massachusetts Bay Colony, where women met to
[discuss the week's sermons](https://www.minnpost.com/books/2009/09/evolution-american-book-clubs-timeline/).
In 1827 the Society of Young Ladies in Lynn, Massachusetts, began a run of
African American women's literary societies across the Northeast. For a long
time a book club was where people kept out of formal education got some
anyway, and I think that's still a fair description of what a good one does.

## Why join one

A survey of 2,000 American book club members,
[commissioned by ThriftBooks](https://www.thriftbooks.com/blog/are-book-clubs-the-new-dating-app-survey-infographic),
asked why they joined. The top answers were reading new books (68%),
socializing (63%) and making new friends (59%), and 28% said they'd struggled
to meet people with similar taste in books before. It's a sponsored poll of
people already in clubs, so it tells you what members say, which isn't the
same as what clubs do.

My own guess at the biggest benefit is the deadline. A book you've promised to
discuss on the 14th gets finished, and a book on your own shelf can wait
forever. I say that as someone whose reading list on the
[Impossible List](/impossible-list/) will comfortably outlive me.

## Is it good for you?

People often say book clubs are good for you. The best-known work comes
from the University of Liverpool, which ran
[Shared Reading groups for adults diagnosed with depression](https://mh.bmj.com/content/38/1/15)
for a year. Depression measures improved while the groups ran, but the
researchers said plainly that a small sample, dropouts and no control group
meant they couldn't call it an effect. A
[2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1484839/full)
of Shared Reading studies found the same pattern: better wellbeing and fewer
depressive symptoms, mostly in small studies with nothing to compare against.
It's promising, and nobody has proven it yet.

## Finding one to join

Start at the library. Many public libraries run book clubs of their own, and
the staff tend to know which local groups want members. Bookstores, Meetup and
Eventbrite come next; in 2026 Eventbrite told
[Axios](https://www.axios.com/newsletters/axios-nw-arkansas-c61e766e-c30c-4a66-9635-08b83af937ee)
that book club events on its platform were up 33% on the year before, driven
mostly by very niche meetups.

Online, the choice comes down to how much structure you want. Goodreads groups
are huge and free. Fable and The StoryGraph both host clubs inside a reading
app. A few times a year OverDrive runs the
[Big Library Read](https://company.overdrive.com/?p=34546), a global ebook
club where one title can be borrowed through Libby by everyone at once, with no
waitlist.

Try two or three before you commit. Every club has its own speed and
seriousness, and a club that reads like a seminar will lose the person who came
for the snacks, and the other way round.

## Starting your own

1. Invite three to five people and ask each of them to bring a friend, which
   [one library recommends](https://newcastlelibraries.com.au/experience/what-s-on/book-club-hub/getting-started)
   as the quick way past your own circle.
2. Pick a regular slot. Monthly is the standard, and
   [every six weeks](https://www.spokanelibrary.org/?p=14885) gives long books
   and slow readers room.
3. Agree at the first meeting how books get chosen. Choosing several months
   ahead gives everyone time to find a copy, and
   [BookBrowse](https://www.bookbrowse.com/bookclubs/advice/index.cfm/fuseaction/first_meetings)
   passes on a rule I like: whoever nominates a book has to have read it.
4. If members don't know each other yet, meet somewhere public the first time,
   like a library room or a café. Bars look fun and are
   [usually too loud](https://bookish.netgalley.com/book-club/04/2022/how-to-start-a-book-club/)
   for a real conversation.
5. Give one person the job of opening the talk with a single open question, and
   pass that job around.

The first book matters more than the rest. If you're the one starting the club,
pick something you've read, liked and can talk about for an hour.

## Tools that help

| Tool | What it's for | Cost |
|------|---------------|------|
| Library book club kits | Usually 8 to 15 copies of one title in a bag, often with discussion questions, lent for about six weeks | Free with a library card |
| Big Library Read | One ebook or audiobook that everyone can borrow at once through Libby, for about two weeks at a time | Free with a library card |
| Bookclubs | Scheduling, book polls, RSVPs and reminders for a club you run | Free, with extra features on paid plans |
| Fable | Online clubs with spoiler-free discussion rooms for each chapter | Free, with a paid Plus tier |
| The StoryGraph | Clubs with a built-in forum, inside a reading tracker | Free, with a paid Plus tier |
| Goodreads groups | Large discussion groups on almost any niche | Free |

Kits vary by library, so check your own catalogue; some, like
[Halifax Public Libraries](https://www.halifaxpubliclibraries.ca/adults/book-club-kits/),
keep more than two hundred titles. If the group is spread out, a video call
does the job of the living room. And for the night itself, a kit's discussion
guide or a list of general questions that fit any book will get a quiet room
talking.

## So what's a book club for?

Mostly it's for finishing the book, and then hearing what a handful of other
people saw in it that you missed. The case that it's good for your health is
still small studies and good intentions, and the history says the best clubs
were always about getting something people weren't otherwise offered. If
you're one of the 93% who has wondered, the library is the cheapest place to
find out whether you'd like being in the club.
