This page is for the days when I know exactly what I should be doing and I'm
doing something else. Two videos, from two very different people, arrive at
the same instruction: move first, and let the feeling catch up. These are my
notes, kept short on purpose, so the page is quick to reread when I need it.

## You already know enough

The first video asks why the smartest person in the room is so rarely the one
getting things done. The creator's answer is that the more you know, the more
ways you can see to fail, so you plan, research and take one more course instead of
starting. Learning feels productive, and you can't fail at something you
haven't tried.

The creator started a YouTube channel at 15 with no skill and no strategy,
just a belief that posting more would work, and learned more by posting than
research could have taught. Now, with 1.4 million subscribers and a head full
of strategy, the overthinking has set in on every video. A
mediocre plan carried out beats a perfect one that never starts.

## Act first, feel it later

The second video is from someone who spent years drifting without a passion
and decided to force one. The core move has a name, behavioral activation:
stop waiting to feel motivated before you act, because the motivation shows up
after you start. "I'll do it tomorrow" just reschedules the excuse.

If you have to drag your feet to a hobby you say you enjoy, he counts that as
doing it right. You still went.

## Swap the goal for an experiment

Goals are pass or fail, and most of them hang on things you don't control,
which is exactly what freezes an overthinker. The first video borrows a fix
from a book called *Tiny Experiments*: notice what you're curious about, guess
what might work, then commit to a pact that's purposeful, actionable,
continuous and trackable. The format is one line, "I will *action* for
*duration*." Don't judge it halfway. When it ends, ask how it felt and what you
learned, then keep going or design the next one.

The creator's own experiment is three-day work sprints at the library, then
four days off, instead of forcing the same output every day. It's held as a
hypothesis, which is the point: every result is data, so there's nothing to
fail.

## Give it ten minutes

On days you really don't want to, set a timer for ten minutes and do the thing
until it rings. If you still hate it, stop. Usually your brain warms up and ten
minutes turns into forty.

## Get good, then love it

The passion video leans on Cal Newport's *So Good They Can't Ignore You*:
people who love their work rarely started with passion. They got good, and the
passion followed. We like what we're good at, and everything starts with being
bad at it, so sucking at first doesn't mean it isn't for you. Comparison is the
thief of joy.

## Play, and lower the stakes

Kids learn the world through play, and adults lose it by turning every new
hobby into a syllabus. Make the first goals easy to hit. If it's piano, just
mess around on the piano for a few days before touching theory.

## Clear the deck and look back

Take the phone out of the room. Infinite scrolling raises the bar for what
feels interesting until ordinary things feel boring, and the video's first fix
is simply distance from it. Then look back for what you loved before anyone
was grading you. Someone who loved toy cars as a kid might go to a car meet
alone and say they're new. Or put a few hobbies on a wheel and spin it.

And count what you have. The passion video makes room for people whose lives
are genuinely hard, and says there are people out there waiting to be your
friend. If it's more than a slump, the video says it plainly and so will I:
talk to someone qualified.

## The pocket version

- Move first. The feeling comes second.
- You know enough to start. The next course is a hiding place.
- A mediocre plan, carried out, beats a perfect one in a drawer.
- Swap the goal for a pact: "I will *this* for *that long*." Then look at the data.
- A skip is information. Ask why.
- Ten minutes. Then you're allowed to stop.
- Passion follows skill. Being bad at first is the entry fee.
- Lower the stakes until it's play again.
- Phone out of the room.
- Comparison is the thief of joy.
