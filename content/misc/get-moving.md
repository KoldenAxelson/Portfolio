This page is for the days when I know exactly what I should be doing and I'm
doing something else. It's a bucket: six videos so far, most of them saying
the same few things in different words, boiled down to one set of notes. The
instruction they share is to move first and let the feeling catch up, and
most of the rest is about making the first move cheap. I keep it short so it's
quick to reread when I need it.

## You already know enough

Smart people are so rarely the ones getting things done because the more you
know, the more ways you can see to fail. So you plan, research and take one
more course instead of starting. Learning feels productive, and you can't
fail at something you haven't tried.

One creator started a YouTube channel at 15 with no skill and no strategy and
learned more from posting than research could have taught. Now, with 1.4
million subscribers and a head full of strategy, the overthinking has set in. A mediocre plan carried out beats a perfect one that never starts.

## Act first, feel it later

Stop waiting to feel motivated. The move has a name, behavioral activation:
the motivation shows up once you've started. If you have to drag your
feet to a hobby you say you enjoy, that counts as doing it right, because you
still went.

If it's more than a slump, the advice in these videos isn't the tool for it.
Talk to someone qualified.

## Willpower is the wrong tool

Acting first is hard because the cost of a hard thing arrives now and the
reward arrives later. One creator explains it as the brain's reward system
discounting the future steeply, so telling yourself to think about long-term
goals asks willpower to overrule something hardwired. Willpower is expensive
and fades when you're stressed or tired. The most disciplined people have
mostly stopped needing it.

So make the right thing cheaper in the moment. Pair the hard task with a treat
you only allow during it, which Milkman's research calls temptation bundling:
a playlist saved for runs, a fancy iced coffee you only drink while studying.
Do it often enough and the treat becomes a cue, and making the coffee starts
warming your brain up for the session.

## Make it easy to start

James Clear's four laws are the same idea as a checklist: make a habit
obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying. Leave the training clothes where you
can see them, have the bag packed and the session picked, and mark it done
afterward so you can see the progress. For a habit you want less of, run the
laws backward until it's hard to reach.

## Keep small promises

This is the idea on the page I think about most. Every promise to yourself
that you break teaches you to expect the same ending next time, like a friend
who keeps not showing up at six. One creator calls it your brain's trust
score, and you raise it with promises small enough to keep, like two pages
after dinner. Commit one week at a time rather than forever, and when you
slip, follow Clear's rule: never miss twice.

## Swap the goal for an experiment

Goals are pass or fail, and many hang on things you don't control, which is
what freezes an overthinker. The book *Tiny Experiments* swaps them for pacts:
notice what you're curious about, guess what might work, then commit for a
fixed length. The format is one line, "I will *action* for *duration*." When
it ends, ask how it felt and what you learned, then keep going or design the
next one.

One creator's experiment is three-day work sprints at the library, then four
days off, held as a hypothesis so every result is data. A one-week pact and a
one-week commitment are the same thing under two names.

## Give it ten minutes

On days you really don't want to, set a timer for ten minutes and do the thing
until it rings. If you still hate it, stop. Usually your brain warms up and
ten minutes turns into thirty or forty.

## Repeat the boring thing

What's usually missing is repetition: the same boring basics, done a thousand
times, in a world of next-day delivery that has trained us to expect results
fast. One creator made her first thousand online after nearly two years, and
none of her videos took off until she'd posted for nearly a year and a half.
When the wait feels unbearable, ask what the alternative is. Quit, and you
keep the job or the weight you were trying to leave.

The wait is preparation, the stretch where you become someone patient enough
to have earned the result. The line I'd tape to a monitor: stop being mad at
the results you didn't get from the work you didn't do.

## Get good, then love it

Cal Newport's *So Good They Can't Ignore You* makes the case that people who
love their work rarely started with passion. They got good, and the passion
followed. Everything starts with being bad at it, so sucking at first doesn't
mean it isn't for you. Keep it playful, too: if it's piano, mess around on the
piano for a few days before you touch theory.

## Notice what the scroll is for

This one landed closer to home than I'd like. A lot of what feels like a treat
is anaesthesia: you eat when you aren't hungry and scroll when you aren't
curious, and the tell is the guilt afterward. Before the reach, ask what you
actually need right now, or what you're avoiding. Then pick the slower
pleasure when you can.

Taking the phone out of the room is the small version. The big version is one
creator who says she's shopped once this year and has no social media besides
YouTube, so her brain stops expecting everything instantly.

## Give each part of the day a job

Five tasks written down for "later" end up fighting over the same hour at nine
at night. Split the day into morning, afternoon and evening, give each part
one main job, and treat each as a fresh start. A bad morning still leaves two.

## The pocket version

- Move first. The feeling comes second.
- You know enough to start. The next course is a hiding place.
- Make the right thing cheap and the wrong thing far away.
- Promise small and keep it. Never miss twice.
- Swap the goal for a pact: "I will *this* for *that long*."
- Ten minutes, then you're allowed to stop.
- Repeat the boring basics. The wait is part of the work.
- Before the scroll or the snack, ask what you actually need.
- A bad morning still leaves the afternoon.

## Source

Six videos, written up in October 2026. Everything above is my synthesis, and
the personal details are the speakers' own. Only the self-improvement video
names its speaker in the transcript (James), so I haven't guessed the others.

The discipline video's neuroscience (dopamine as a present-biased system,
clutter crowding working memory) is her explanation, and I haven't checked it.
The pleasure video quotes Carl Jung throughout, but at least one of those
quotes is a known misattribution and I couldn't trace another to his writing, so I
kept its ideas and left the quotes. I also left out the passion video's
section on cannabis, ADHD medication and dopamine, which is medical territory
I can't check, and the self-improvement video's blocking setup.

- [Why You're Too Smart To Be This Broke](https://youtu.be/OV4ZuQQO2v4): the knowing-doing gap, learning by doing, and the tiny-experiment pact.
- [How to force passion](https://youtu.be/IznpzDrjaGo): behavioral activation, ten-minute timers, Cal Newport, and play.
- [You'll Never Look at Pleasure the Same After This](https://youtu.be/PXbf0jpS2g4): pleasure as anaesthesia, the guilt afterward, and the question to ask before the scroll.
- [How to trick your brain into becoming so disciplined](https://youtu.be/DzfF5SEzpWE): why willpower is the wrong tool, temptation bundling, cues and environment.
- [7 years of self improvement lessons in 4 min](https://youtu.be/Fdc1E0VjbZc): the trust score, the graduation method, never miss twice, the four laws, and a day in three parts.
- [This is boring but you'll outwork everyone](https://youtu.be/yDQPa-IVdTA): repetition over new methods, "what's the alternative?", and the wait as preparation.
